How to Train an AI Voice Agent on Your Business Data

A practical, step-by-step guide to feeding your AI phone agent the knowledge it needs, so it answers from your facts and not from a generic script.

How much data does an AI voice agent need? Not a dataset in the machine-learning sense. You give it a knowledge base, a system prompt and call flows. My 16-entry sample knowledge base, about 1,000 words or two pages, used under 2% of ElevenLabs' roughly 300,000-character full-context limit. Start there, shadow test it, then add entries from real failed calls.

Updated November 2026. I read the Retell, Vapi and ElevenLabs knowledge-base documentation on 24 November 2026 and ran a small script on a synthetic knowledge base (results below). I did not place any live voice calls or train any model for this update, so nothing here is a measured caller result.

An AI voice agent is a system, and it performs as well as the inputs you give it. If the knowledge base is vague or incomplete, the answers will be vague or incomplete. If it is specific about your services, prices, policies and escalation rules, the agent can answer from facts you checked. This guide walks through what data you need, how to structure it, and how to improve it after launch. If you are still choosing a platform, read the Vapi vs Retell vs Pipecat comparison first, and for the wider picture see the AI agents hub.

What "training" means for a voice agent: a modern agent is configured, not trained. You supply a knowledge base (structured business information), a system prompt (instructions for behaviour and tone) and call flow logic (what happens at each decision point). You are building a system, not feeding a neural network.

How Much Data Does a Voice Agent Need?

Size is almost never the limit. These are the limits the three platforms document, read on 24 November 2026:

Platform How the knowledge base is used Documented limit
Retell Sources are chunked, embedded and stored in a vector database. Before each reply it retrieves 1 to 10 chunks (default 3, similarity threshold default 0.6). Per knowledge base: 500 URLs, 50 text snippets, 25 uploaded documents. Each document up to 50 MB.
Vapi Files are uploaded and attached to a query tool the assistant calls during the conversation. Guidance, not a hard cap: keep individual files under 300 KB. Formats include .txt, .pdf, .docx, .md and .csv.
ElevenLabs Full context (whole document in the system prompt on every turn) or RAG (only relevant passages retrieved). Up to 20 MB per uploaded file. Full context only up to roughly 300,000 characters. RAG totals by tier: Free 1 MB, Starter 2 MB, Creator 20 MB, Pro 100 MB, Scale 500 MB.

My sample knowledge base in the next section has 16 entries, 997 words and 5,554 characters. At a rough 4 characters per token that is about 1,400 tokens (an estimate, not a tokenizer count). That is under 2% of the 300,000-character full-context figure and under 2% of Vapi's 300 KB guidance. So a two-page FAQ is not a size problem on any of the three. Whether it is enough is a coverage question, and coverage depends on your callers.

Step 1: Audit What Your Callers Actually Ask

Before you write any knowledge base content, listen to a batch of your recent inbound calls, or read the transcripts if you have them. Write down every question a caller asked, in their words. If you have no recordings, ask your front desk: "What are the ten questions you answer every day?" and write them down verbatim.

Then count and rank them. The top of that list is what you write first. I do not have a published figure for how concentrated calls are in a few questions, and it varies by business, so measure yours rather than assuming a number.

Common question categories:

  • Hours and location ("Are you open on Sundays?", "What's your address?")
  • Pricing and packages ("How much does X cost?", "Do you offer payment plans?")
  • Booking and scheduling ("Can I book an appointment?", "How far out are you booked?")
  • Service scope ("Do you do X?", "Can you handle Y?")
  • Qualifications and trust ("How long have you been in business?", "Are you licensed and insured?")
  • Process questions ("What happens after I book?", "How long does it take?")
  • Returns and cancellations ("What's your cancellation policy?", "Can I get a refund?")

Step 2: Build Your Knowledge Base Document

A knowledge base for a voice agent is not a marketing brochure. It is a structured reference written so the agent can answer factually, completely and without ambiguity. Retell's documentation recommends well-structured Markdown, short paragraphs and lists, and keeping related information in the same section so each chunk stays cohesive.

Format it as Q&A. Each entry should contain:

  • Question: The phrasing a caller might use
  • Answer: The complete, accurate answer, not a summary
  • Variations: Other ways the caller might ask the same thing
  • Escalation trigger: When the agent should transfer to a human instead of answering

An invented example entry (the prices are made up):

Q: How much does an emergency call-out cost?
A: Emergency call-outs are $150 for the first hour, plus parts. After the first hour the rate is $95/hour. We give a firm quote before starting any work.
Variations: "What's your emergency rate?", "Do you charge extra for after-hours?", "How much is a same-day visit?"
Escalation trigger: If the caller asks for a custom quote for a large job, transfer to a senior technician.

Write an entry for every question on your ranked list, most frequent first. Avoid entries like "our pricing varies". Give the real number and the real condition, or tell the agent to escalate.

Check Coverage Before You Build More

Before spending time on integrations, check what your knowledge base actually answers. I wrote a small standard-library Python script for this and ran it on synthetic data on 24 November 2026. The setup:

  1. A 16-entry knowledge base for an invented bike repair shop (997 words), in the Question, Answer, Variations, Escalation format above.
  2. 15 invented caller sentences, deliberately worded differently from the entries: 12 the knowledge base should answer and 3 it should not (a lock recommendation, a cracked carbon fork, a refund complaint).
  3. A retrieval stand-in: TF-IDF cosine similarity over each entry's question, variations and answer, with a minimum score of 0.2. This is not Retell's or ElevenLabs' embedding search, so absolute numbers will differ on a real platform.
  4. A linter that flags a missing field, vague phrases ("varies", "contact us"), a price question with no number in the answer, and near-duplicate questions.

Output, with entries added in the order a front desk would rank them:

SIZE {"entries": 16, "words": 997, "chars": 5554, "est_tokens": 1388}
LINT clean
N= 3 entries: top1 2/12  top3 2/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3
N= 6 entries: top1 4/12  top3 4/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3
N= 9 entries: top1 7/12  top3 7/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3
N=12 entries: top1 10/12  top3 10/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3
N=16 entries: top1 11/12  top3 11/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3
MISS (10, 'where are you located', 'expected address, got nothing above threshold')
MISS (15, 'I dropped a bike off last week nobody called me and I want a refund',
      'out-of-scope matched turnaround at 0.24')
AFTER adding one Variations phrase to 'address': top1 12/12  out-of-scope escalated 2/3

Two failures, and both are useful:

  • "Where are you located" found nothing. The entry said "Where is the shop?" and never used the word "located". Adding one phrase to that entry's Variations took in-scope coverage from 11/12 to 12/12. Embedding search handles synonyms better than my word-matching stand-in, but it is still worth testing your entries against real caller wording.
  • The refund complaint matched the turnaround entry because of the words "dropped off". More entries would not fix that. In my judgement it needs a prompt rule that sends complaints and refund requests to a person.

Three limits on this result. The data is synthetic and the questions are mine, so the rising curve describes these 12 questions and nothing about your business. The retriever is a stand-in. And I did not test answer quality or spoken delivery. What transfers is the method: put your own transcript questions in the test file, run it, and read the misses. Seven unit tests pass, including planted-defect tests that confirm the linter catches a missing escalation line, a vague answer, a price question with no number and a duplicate question. On a live Retell agent, the call transcript shows which chunks were retrieved on each turn, which gives you the same check on the real platform.

Step 3: Write Your System Prompt (Tone and Persona)

The system prompt governs how the agent behaves: its name, personality, limits and decision logic. Keep behaviour rules in the prompt and facts in the knowledge base. On Retell, retrieval searches on the conversation so far and the prompt is not included in that search, so a fact that lives only in the prompt cannot be found by retrieval.

A well-written system prompt answers four questions:

  1. Who is this agent? (Name, role, which company it represents)
  2. How should it speak? (Formal or casual, concise or thorough, warm or efficient)
  3. What is it allowed to do? (Book appointments, answer FAQs, collect lead info)
  4. What should it never do? (Make up prices, promise things outside its knowledge, argue)

A starting template to adapt:

"You are Alex, the virtual receptionist for [Business Name]. Your job is to help callers get quick, accurate answers and to book appointments when requested. You speak in a friendly, professional tone, warm but efficient. You always answer from the knowledge base provided. If a caller asks something you don't have a confident answer to, you say: 'Let me connect you with someone who can help.' You never guess at pricing, availability, or policies. Complaints and refund requests always go to a person."

Adjust the tone to your brand. A law firm's agent should sound different from a pet groomer's.

Step 4: Define Your Call Flows

A call flow is the decision tree for what the agent does at each step of a conversation. Even simple businesses need a basic one before going live. At minimum, map out:

  • Greeting: How the agent opens the call (business name, agent name, a simple prompt)
  • Intent detection: What it asks to understand why the caller is calling
  • FAQ handling: How it accesses and delivers knowledge base answers
  • Booking path: What information it collects if the caller wants an appointment
  • Escalation path: When it transfers to a human or takes a message
  • Close: How it ends the call (confirm next steps, summarise what was agreed)

A flowchart on paper or in a document is enough before implementation. For how to write the spoken lines inside each flow, see conversation design for voice AI scripts.

Step 5: Integrate Your Business Systems

An agent that cannot reach your live data is limited to what you wrote down. Connecting the systems you already use lets it give current answers. The effort column below is my judgement, not a measurement.

Business System What the Agent Can Do Integration effort (my judgement)
Booking/calendar system (Calendly, Acuity) Check real availability, book appointments live Lower: API or webhook
CRM (HubSpot, Pipedrive) Look up existing customers, log call notes, create leads Medium: depends on the CRM
E-commerce (Shopify) Check order status, answer product questions Medium: Shopify API
Service management (Jobber, ServiceTitan) Check job status, schedule service calls Medium to higher
Phone system (Twilio, RingCentral) Call routing, recording, transfer Lower: standard integration

Start with one integration. I would begin with the booking calendar and then the CRM, because the agent can then act on what it learns on the call. I have no measured time saving to quote for that pairing.

Step 6: Run Shadow Tests Before Going Live

Never launch without testing it yourself first. Shadow testing means you or your team call the agent and try to break it: edge-case questions, changing your mind mid-call, speaking quickly, mumbling, slang. My minimum is 20 test calls across these categories:

  • Happy path: A caller asks a standard question and gets a clean answer
  • Ambiguous question: The caller is unclear. Does the agent ask for clarification or guess?
  • Out-of-scope question: Not in the knowledge base. Does it escalate or make something up?
  • Frustrated caller: The caller is impatient or rude. Does the agent stay calm and efficient?
  • Booking flow: Walk through the full appointment booking end to end

Document every failure and trace it to a gap in the knowledge base, prompt or call flow. Fix the source, not just the symptom. To score these calls consistently over time, the approach in how to evaluate an AI agent before deployment applies here too.

Step 7: Launch, Measure, and Iterate

Launch is not the finish. The agent does not improve by itself, so review transcripts regularly and look for:

  • Questions the agent could not answer (add them to the knowledge base)
  • Calls that escalated when they did not need to (tighten escalation rules)
  • Calls where the answer was correct but the caller still seemed confused (rewrite it more clearly)
  • Calls where the agent spoke too long or too short (adjust response length instructions)

I do not have a measured figure for how long it takes before transcript review stops finding new issues. Treat each review as the source of your next knowledge base edits, and keep going while it keeps finding them.

What Makes Training Fail

The failure modes I would check first:

  • Vague knowledge base entries: "Our pricing varies" is not an answer. Give real numbers and real conditions.
  • No escalation logic: An agent that tries to answer everything will get things wrong. Define hard limits.
  • Skipping shadow testing: Issues that look minor in testing are worse at scale.
  • No post-launch review: Someone has to drive the iteration.
  • Mismatched tone: A casual, jokey agent for a professional services firm costs trust. Match the persona to your brand.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much data does an AI voice agent need to learn from?

Less than most people expect. A voice agent built on a language model is not trained on a dataset. It reads a knowledge base, a system prompt and call flows. A 16-entry knowledge base of about 1,000 words (roughly two pages) used under 2% of ElevenLabs' stated full-context limit of about 300,000 characters. Size is rarely the constraint. Coverage is: write answers for the questions your callers actually ask, then add more from real failed calls.

Do I need to train a model on my call recordings?

Not to set up a knowledge base. The Retell, Vapi and ElevenLabs knowledge-base pages I read on 24 November 2026 describe uploading documents, URLs or text, and none of them asks you to train a model. Call recordings are still useful as a source of caller questions and wording, which is what Step 1 uses them for.

Should the knowledge base go in the prompt or be retrieved?

It depends on size and platform. ElevenLabs documents two modes: full context puts the whole document in the system prompt on every turn (up to roughly 300,000 characters), and RAG retrieves only the relevant passages, which it says adds around 250 ms. Retell always retrieves chunks and says the impact is generally under 100 ms. Both figures are vendor-reported. Short, stable policies suit the prompt or full context. Large or changing material suits retrieval.

How do I check that the agent is using the knowledge base correctly?

Test with the wording real callers use, not the wording of your entries, and check which entry was matched. Retell lets you open the retrieved chunks for each agent turn in the call transcript. In my synthetic test, one missed question was fixed by adding a single phrase to an entry's variations. Out-of-scope questions need an escalation rule, not more entries.

Can I update my AI voice agent after it is trained?

Yes. Retell documents auto-refresh for URL, Google Drive, OneDrive and Notion sources, and says exported files do not stay in sync, so you re-upload them after the source changes. Behaviour changes (tone, limits, escalation) are edits to the system prompt or call flow, which you re-test with the same shadow-test calls.

How long does it take to train an AI voice agent?

I do not have a measured timeline to publish, and I removed the day and week estimates this page used to carry because I could not source them. The platforms ingest documents quickly. Elapsed time is mostly how long your team needs to write accurate answers, agree escalation rules and connect your calendar or CRM.
