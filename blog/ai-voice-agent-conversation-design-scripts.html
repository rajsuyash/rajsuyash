AI Voice Agent Conversation Design: How to Write Scripts That Sound Natural and Convert

The difference between an AI voice agent that builds trust and one that frustrates callers comes down to conversation design. Here is how to get it right from the start.

Design a voice AI script as five steps, not a menu tree: a short greeting that names the business and says it is an AI, one open question for intent, only the fields needed to act, a read-back before booking, and a clean finish or a handoff with a summary. Keep each turn to one or two sentences.

Updated November 2026. I re-read the Vapi and Retell prompting guides on 13 November 2026, and I ran a small script linter on two synthetic scripts (results below). I did not place any live voice calls for this update, so nothing here is a measured caller result.

Most businesses that deploy an AI voice agent spend their energy on the platform: which vendor, which CRM integration, where to host it. Those are fair questions. The script is the part you control completely, and it is where a deployment is most likely to go wrong. A badly written script frustrates callers whichever platform runs it. What Conversation Design Means for AI Voice Agents

Conversation design is the practice of deciding what an AI agent says, when it says it, how it reacts to different caller inputs, and how it recovers when a call goes sideways. It is part copywriting, part information architecture and part UX design, applied to spoken dialogue.

It is not scripting a phone tree. Legacy IVR systems used rigid menus: "Press 1 for appointments, press 2 for billing." An LLM-based voice agent understands natural language, so a caller can say "I need to move my appointment from Thursday" and no button is pressed. Your job is to define the goal of each type of call, the information the agent needs, and what the end of the call looks like, not to map every possible path.

The Anatomy of an Effective Voice Agent Script

Every working call I have seen described in vendor docs follows the same shape, whatever the business:

  1. Greeting and identity disclosure: state the business name, say it is an AI assistant, invite the caller to speak.
  2. Intent capture: understand what the caller wants, from a direct statement or one clarifying question.
  3. Information gathering: collect only the fields needed to complete the task.
  4. Confirmation: repeat the key details back before taking any action, especially a booking.
  5. Completion or handoff: finish the request and close cleanly, or transfer to a person with a short summary.

In my reading, the first two steps are where scripts most often break: greetings that run long so callers talk over them, and intent capture that only recognises one phrasing. That is a judgement from the documentation and from the patterns in the table below, not a measured failure rate.

Writing greetings that work

A greeting has one job: tell the caller who they reached and invite them to speak. An example: "Thanks for calling Greenfield Services, I'm an AI assistant. How can I help you today?" That is 15 words, about 6 seconds at an assumed 150 words a minute. A greeting that recites services, hours and a web address buries the invitation. I use three elements:

  • The business name, so callers know they reached the right place.
  • An AI disclosure in the first sentence.
  • One open question ("How can I help you today?"), not a list of options.

Disclosure: what I verified and what I did not

Article 50(1) of the EU AI Act says providers must ensure AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed so those persons are informed they are interacting with an AI system, unless that is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person given the circumstances. I read the article text on artificialintelligenceact.eu on 13 November 2026, which gives 2 August 2026 as the date Article 50 applies under Article 113. Handling intent without a menu

With an LLM-based agent you do not train a classifier on utterances. You describe each intent and the action it triggers in the prompt, and you add a few example exchanges. Vapi's prompting guide lists "provide few-shot examples" as its own step, and Retell's example prompt writes the call as a numbered flow with one step per phase. If a caller says "I want to book for next Tuesday", "Can I come in this week?" or "I need an appointment", all three should land in the same booking step. The way to find out whether they do is to test them, which is the next section.

Two things to take from it. The vendors agree on short turns and one question at a time. Five Conversation Flows to Start From

I cannot give you a figure for how much of your call volume these five cover, and I removed the percentage this page used to state because it had no source. Count your own calls first, then build the flows your logs justify. These five are a starting set.

1. Appointment booking

Collect the service type (if there is more than one), the preferred day and time, the caller's name, and a phone number or email for the confirmation. Check availability in the scheduling system and book in the same call. Do not ask for an address, date of birth or insurance details unless the appointment cannot be held without them.

2. Hours, location and general information

Short calls. State the answer, check the caller has what they need, offer further help and let them go.

3. Pricing and services

If pricing is fixed and public, state it. If it depends on scope, collect enough context to give a range or route to a person for a quote. Give a clear answer or offer a booking; do not bury the caller in caveats.

4. Rescheduling and cancellations

A caller who cancels may be ready to reschedule. Confirm the cancellation, then offer the next available slot in the same turn. It costs one sentence.

5. Escalation to a human

Treat it as a designed path, not a failure. A handoff line such as "Let me connect you with a team member. I'll pass on what we've covered so you don't repeat yourself" only works if the context really is passed along. Define the triggers in advance: complaints you cannot resolve, requests that need a person's judgement, and any caller who asks for a human. Do not escalate what the agent can handle; it adds friction.

Script Patterns: What Works and What Loses Callers

The table below is my own working checklist. The thresholds are rules of thumb, apart from the two I attribute to Vapi in the lint section.

Script Element Ineffective Pattern Effective Pattern
Greeting length Recites services, hours and a website About 20 words or fewer: name, AI disclosure, open question
Intent capture "Please say one of the following: appointment, billing, directions, or general inquiry" "How can I help you today?" and route from the natural reply
Information gathering Asking for 8 fields before confirming any availability 2 to 3 fields before confirming the slot is bookable
Confirmation Booking without reading back details "I have you down for Thursday, July 31st at 10 am. Does that work?"
Silence Repeating the same prompt three times, then disconnecting One check-in, then offer a callback or a person
Unknown intent "I did not understand that. Please try again." "I want to make sure I get you to the right place. Are you calling to book an appointment, or is it something else?"
Cancellations Confirming the cancellation and ending the call Confirming it, then offering the next open slot
Escalation handoff Transferring with no context, so the caller starts over Transferring with a short summary of intent and what was captured

I Turned the Table into a Linter

A checklist is easy to nod along to and easy to ignore. So I wrote the table as code: a 12-check linter that reads a script spec (the agent's turns plus a few flow settings) and fails any rule it breaks. I ran it on two synthetic scripts for a made-up business, one written in the legacy IVR style from the table's left column and one in the right column's style. Python 3.12.3, standard library only, no voice platform involved.

The thresholds sit at the top of the file. Two come from the Vapi guide (turn length, one question per turn) and one from its unclear-input example (at most two attempts). The rest, including the 20-word greeting and the 150 words a minute used to turn words into seconds, are my assumptions.

MAX_GREETING_WORDS = 20      # about 8 s at the ASSUMED 150 words/min
MAX_SENTENCES_PER_TURN = 2   # Vapi guide: "one or two sentences maximum"
MAX_QUESTIONS_PER_TURN = 1   # Vapi guide: "Ask only one question at a time"
MAX_FIELDS_BEFORE_AVAILABILITY = 3
MAX_REPROMPTS = 2

def sentences(text):
    return [s for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text.strip()) if s]

# inside lint(spec):
long_turns = [k for k, v in turns.items() if len(sentences(v)) > MAX_SENTENCES_PER_TURN]
chk("C5 every turn at most 2 sentences", not long_turns, ...)

It earned its place on the first run. My first draft of the revised greeting was three sentences ("Thanks for calling Greenfield Services. I'm an AI assistant. How can I help you today?"), and check C5 failed it against the two-sentence rule. I merged the first two sentences and the revised script went from 11 of 12 to 12 of 12. What this shows and what it does not. The legacy script scoring 1 of 12 and the revised one 12 of 12 is by construction, since I wrote each to match its column. It proves the rules are checkable, not that the rules improve outcomes. It reads text; it cannot hear pacing, interruptions or a bad transcription. And it knows nothing about caller completion or satisfaction, which I did not measure.

The Mistakes That Cost Caller Trust

Over-scripting edge cases

People who have run a phone line for years want to script every case: the disputed charge from three months ago, the caller who is upset, the question about a service discontinued last year. The result is a long prompt. Retell's guide is blunt that a longer prompt is not a better prompt: it says every extra instruction makes an agent a little worse at following the rest, and it tells you to cut repetition. Write the common paths well, give the agent a clear fallback for what it does not know, and route the rest to a person.

Robotic affirmations

"Great!", "Absolutely!" and "Of course!" after every reply sound artificial by the third repetition. Read the script aloud. If it sounds like a motivational poster, rewrite it.

Asking for information you do not need

Each required field is one more thing the caller must know and say. Collect what is needed to hold the slot and gather the rest at the appointment.

Ignoring what happens after the call

A confirmation text with the time, address and cancellation terms is part of the design. Design the whole loop, not only the dialogue.

Testing Before You Go Live

Static checks like the linter catch structural problems. They do not tell you how callers actually phrase things. Vapi's guide says to validate prompt changes against a representative test set, not single calls, because probabilistic regressions only show up across many runs. My own starting point, which is a habit and not a researched benchmark:

  1. Call it yourself with about ten scripted scenarios: easy bookings, a reschedule the system cannot find, a question outside what you offer. Note every awkward or robotic moment.
  2. Have about five people who have never heard the script call it cold. Give each a scenario and nothing else. Watch where they get stuck.
  3. Test escalation on purpose: a caller who asks for a person reaches one, the context arrives, and the person receiving the call knows what to do with it.
  4. Review transcripts every week for the first month. New phrasings and unexpected questions show up in real traffic, and each one is a script change.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should conversation scripts be designed for voice AI?

Write the script as five steps, not a menu tree: an opener that names the business and says it is an AI, open intent capture, only the fields needed to act, a read-back before any booking, and a clean finish or a handoff that passes a summary. Keep each agent turn to one or two sentences with one question.

How long should an AI voice agent greeting be?

I aim for about 20 words or fewer: the business name, an AI disclosure and one open question. The greeting in the lint example below is 15 words, about 6 seconds at an assumed 150 words a minute. That is my rule of thumb, not a measured threshold, and I have no data on caller drop-off by greeting length.

Should an AI voice agent say it is an AI?

I put the disclosure in the first sentence by default. In the EU, Article 50(1) of the AI Act requires AI systems that interact directly with people to be designed so those people are informed they are talking to an AI, unless that is obvious from the context. I did not verify US federal or state rules for this update, so check the rules for every country you call.

How many conversation flows do I need before going live?

Start with the flows your own call logs show. The five on this page (booking, hours and general information, pricing, rescheduling and cancellation, escalation to a human) are a starting set. I have no source for what share of calls they cover, so count your own calls before you trust any percentage.

How do I test voice agent scripts before going live?

Test against a set of scenarios, not single calls: Vapi's prompting guide says to validate changes against a representative test set because regressions only show up across many runs. My starting point is ten scripted scenarios plus five callers who have never heard the script, then weekly transcript review for the first month.
